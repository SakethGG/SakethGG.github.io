This is about the unglamorous half of experimental work: the screws, nuts, the bench and the pace. Not the physics, but the habits that decide whether your physics ever gets tested.
Sure, the ideology should be driven by physics-informed decisions, and another factor can be the competence to fix things and move forward. You need to have a complete understanding and know what you are doing and why you are doing certain things. Definitely, first-principles thinking can help, and maybe we can even figure out a better way to do things. But the most underrated and overlooked components are organization, avoiding work-arounds and a steady pace.

## a. Be a Tortoise

Slow and steady definitely wins the race in the experimental regard. You might think doing things quickly can save you a lot of time and that you can complete another set of experiments or work — sure, you might save some time, but keeping it slow, knowing where you place your equipment and properly ensuring each and every standard is met is even more important. It saves a ton of time in the future and actually gives you better results.

For example, say you have a mechanical setup and it takes 6 screws to couple it to a different fixture or part. Many people just bolt in 3 screws instead, being lazy and thinking they can get it done quickly and start with the experiment. This is completely wrong, and let me tell you why. I believe in engineering there is no subjective decision, only objective ones — every choice was made for a reason, even if the reason isn't documented. So while designing the mechanical setup, the designer might have put a lot of thought into where to put the hole, at what distance from the edge and at what radius from the centre, and also how many to put based on the load-carrying capacity of the screws (which depends on yield strength).

Now, let's say I am only using 3 instead of all 6. I may be fine for now, but as we proceed with the experiment the part might break, and this puts us way behind in our timeline. We may have to completely start from scratch or get the part manufactured again, which delays our work. But taking time to assess our decisions and question them keeps the setup functional and clean.

## b. A Cluttered Desk Is a Cluttered Mind

Make sure your experimental setup is clean and organized. Leaving tools on the work desk is convenient but can be disruptive later. When leaving tools near the setup, especially where electronics are used like PCBs or electrical wires, it is essential to keep them away from metal surfaces like screws, open live wires and metal plates, as they will short instantly and be unusable. This is again an irritating thing.

Also, having assigned spots for tools is best. This will help you find them in the future and also helps your peers in the lab find them. It also helps reduce the inattentional blindness phenomenon.

## c. No Shortcuts

Never settle for a work-around. Like I said, everything in engineering is done for a reason. If the work-around method worked perfectly, why is it called a work-around in the first place? They can damage your setup or product and disrupt your entire flow. Sure, it might be the cheaper option or the available one, but make sure it is not a permanent fix.

Last but not least, document everything and record your results, whether good or bad. Analyse your findings and understand them. Always ask for help when needed.
